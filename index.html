The idea

Heights from angles

Trigonometry lets you find the heights of towers and buildings from angles. This app measures the angles for real using your phone, then does the working.

  • AB is the building. A is the top, B is the bottom.
  • D is my eye (where I hold the phone). C is the ground below me, so DC = h is my eye height.
  • DE is the horizontal line from my eye to the building. Since DE ∥ CB, EB = DC = h.
  • DA and DB are my lines of sight.
The trigonometry behind it

Quick revision

In a right triangle, for an angle θ:

tan θ = side opposite to θ / side adjacent to θ
θ0°30°45°60°90°
tan θ01/√31√3not defined
  • Angle of elevation: the angle between the horizontal and your line of sight when you look up at something.
  • Angle of depression: the angle between the horizontal and your line of sight when you look down at something.

For angles like 37.4° that aren't in the table, the app just uses the exact tan value (like a scientific calculator would).

Way 1 · No tape needed

Two angles, no measuring tape

I look down at the bottom B (angle of depression α), then up at the top A (angle of elevation θ). Only my eye height h is known.

In right ΔDEB,
tan α = EB / DE = h / DE
⇒ DE = h / tan α
In right ΔDEA,
tan θ = AE / DE
⇒ AE = DE × tan θ
Height of building,
AB = AE + EB = AE + h

The cool part: the distance DE isn't measured at all. The angle of depression and my own height give it to us.

Way 2 · I know the distance

Distance + one angle

If I already measured DE (with a tape or by counting steps), I only need the angle of elevation of the top.

In right ΔDEA,
tan θ = AE / DE
⇒ AE = DE × tan θ
AB = AE + h
Way 3 · Two spots

When you can't see the bottom

Here the angle of elevation of the top is θ₁ from one point, and you move s metres further away in a straight line, where it becomes θ₂. Call my near eye position D and the far one F, so DF = s.

In right ΔAED,
tan θ₁ = AE / DE ⇒ AE = DE tan θ₁  …(1)
In right ΔAEF,
tan θ₂ = AE / (DE + s) ⇒ AE = (DE + s) tan θ₂  …(2)
From (1) and (2),
DE tan θ₁ = (DE + s) tan θ₂
⇒ DE = s tan θ₂ / (tan θ₁ − tan θ₂)
Put DE back in (1) to get AE, then AB = AE + h

Example (like the ones in the book)

From a point on the ground, the angle of elevation of the top of a tower is 30°. On walking 20 m towards the tower, it becomes 60°. Find the height of the tower.

Here h = 0 (angles taken from the ground), s = 20 m,
θ₁ = 60° (near), θ₂ = 30° (far)
DE = 20 × (1/√3) / (√3 − 1/√3)
   = 20 × (1/√3) / (2/√3) = 10 m
AB = DE tan 60° = 10√3 = 17.32 m

Open the Calculator, pick "Two spots", put eye height 0, s = 20, θ₁ = 60, θ₂ = 30, and you'll get 17.32 m. Same answer as the book.

Way 4 · Thing on a wall

Calendars, posters, windows, flags

Here you measure the angle to the bottom and to the top of the thing. Let P be the bottom of the calendar and Q its top. E is the point on the wall at my eye level, so DE is my distance from the wall.

In right ΔDEP,
tan θ₁ = EP / DE ⇒ EP = DE tan θ₁
In right ΔDEQ,
tan θ₂ = EQ / DE ⇒ EQ = DE tan θ₂
Height of the calendar,
PQ = EQ − EP = DE (tan θ₂ − tan θ₁)

If DE isn't known, the app first aims at the floor below the calendar (call it F) and uses tan α = EF / DE = h / DE, the same way as Way 1.

If a point is below my eye, its angle is a depression, so I count it as negative. Then EP comes out negative too, which just means P is below E, and PQ = EQ − EP still works.

Behind the scenes

How does the phone know the angle?

Every phone has a tiny sensor called an accelerometer that feels which way gravity is pulling, kind of like the bubble in a spirit level. From that, the phone knows exactly how much it's tilted. When you aim the camera at something, that tilt is the angle of elevation (looking up) or depression (looking down).

Each time you tap, the app waits half a second so your tap doesn't shake the phone, then averages 1.5 seconds of readings (usually 60 to 90 of them). If your hand was too shaky, it asks you to try again. I got this idea from an open-source app called Basic Air Data Clinometer, which only locks a reading once the phone is steady.

Calibration works like a surveyor's trick called reversal. The phone's sensor is usually off by about half a degree to 2 degrees. The app measures with the back on a wall and then with the screen on the same spot. The wall's lean shows up once as plus and once as minus, so averaging the two cancels it. What's left is the sensor's own error, which then gets subtracted from every reading.

Tips

Getting a good answer

  • Calibrate first. Most phone sensors are off by 0.5° to 2° from the factory. That's the biggest source of error.
  • Don't stand too far away. If the angle to the bottom is tiny (under about 8°), even a small error changes the answer a lot. The app warns you when that happens.
  • For small things like a calendar, stand 1.5 to 3 m from the wall, and between aims just tilt the phone without moving it up or down.
  • Measure your eye height properly. In "No tape" mode the whole answer depends on it.
  • Stand about as far away as the building is tall. Angles around 45° give the least error. Very steep or very flat angles make small mistakes bigger.
  • Tuck your elbows in to keep the phone steady.
  • Zoom in on far-away tops so the dot sits exactly on them.
  • Stand on flat ground, at the same level as the bottom of the building.

What's the ± after the answer? Nobody's hand is perfectly still, and the sensor isn't perfect either. The app recalculates with each angle nudged a little up and down, and the ± is how much the height could be off by.
